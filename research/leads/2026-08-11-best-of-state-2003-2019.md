# Leads: Best of State winners, 2003–2019 (Wayback recovery)

Companion to [`2026-08-11-best-of-state-2020-2026.md`](2026-08-11-best-of-state-2020-2026.md),
which covers the years the live site still exposes. This file covers the years it doesn't.
**Nothing here has been verified** except where a line says otherwise. Every entry is a candidate
for a `wiki/pages/` page, and every "why" is my reading of the award listing plus prior knowledge —
it must be replaced with a cited primary source before anything ships. Work queue, not knowledge.

The recovered rows themselves are in
[`best-of-state-winners-2003-2019.tsv`](best-of-state-winners-2003-2019.tsv) — 3,975 rows,
1,911 distinct winner names, columns `year / division / category / winner / award`.

## How it was recovered

The live site's `past-winners` archive begins at 2020. Everything before that came from the
Internet Archive, via the CDX index (`http://web.archive.org/cdx/search/cdx?url=bestofstate.org*`).
Three different site generations, three different URL patterns:

| Years | Wayback path pattern | Format |
|---|---|---|
| 2010–2019 | `/winner{YEAR}_{division}.html`, ten divisions per year | `Category: Winner` per line (2012 alternates category/winner on separate lines) |
| 2005–2008 | `/?cat_code={XX}` and `/awards-{YEAR}.php` | `Category - Winner` per line |
| 2003 | `/winners.htm` plus `/w-{division}.htm` | table cells, wrapped across lines |

All 100 of the 2010–2019 pages were fetched successfully (`https://web.archive.org/web/{ts}id_/{url}`,
earliest snapshot of each). Wayback throttles hard at roughly one request per second sustained; the
fetch needs backoff and retry or it silently returns empty bodies.

**Still missing: 2002, 2004, 2009, and 2011's full medal list.** For 2004 the Archive holds only
`/WinnersGallery2004/` — eleven statue winners as photo pages with no division labels and no bios
(Cecilia Shan, Craig Jessop, David Houle, Elaine Marshall, Erik Jacobsen, Jeannene Barham, Jim
Wilcox, Ross VanVranken, Russ Olsen, Sandra Richards, Sara Douglass). For 2009 only the statue
winners survive. 2002 — the program's first year — has nothing.

## Calibration — what these years add

The four calibration points in the 2020–2026 file all still hold. Four more, specific to this era:

1. **There are two awards, and the wiki should not treat them alike.** Each division awards many
   bronze **medals** by subcategory, and then one **BOSS** (Best of State Statue) per division,
   selected from among that year's medalists. The statue is a far stronger signal — one winner per
   division per year, chosen against every other medalist in it. The 2020–2026 sweep didn't
   distinguish these. Any `source` page built on this award should.
2. **The David F. Pack pattern starts no later than 2012**, not 2020. He appears 27 times in
   2012–2019 across Actor, Adjunct Professor, College/University Teacher, Recreational Coach,
   Appointed Official, City Planning, and Individual Volunteer. An eight-year run makes the
   "diligent repeat entrant" reading close to certain.
3. **The program has honored people later convicted of major fraud.** Jeremy Johnson won Individual
   Humanitarian in 2010 and was subsequently convicted in federal court in connection with iWorks.
   Patrick Byrne of Overstock won the 2003 Community Development statue, 2012 Individual Volunteer,
   and 2014 Entrepreneur. This is the sharpest available evidence that the award tracks nomination
   and reputation at the time, not durable merit — and it belongs on the source page.
4. **Winner names are not normalized across years.** The same organization appears as `SPECTRUM
   ENGINEERS` / `Spectrum Engineers`, `Reaveley Engineers + Associates` / `Reavely Engineers &
   Associates`, `Staker & Parson` / `Staker and Parson`. Deduplicate on a normalized key, not on
   the raw string, or you will overcount distinct organizations by roughly 20%.

## Tier 1 — strongest leads

### US Synthetic — Orem
2008 Misc. Manufacturing medalist. The single best lead in this file, because it closes a gap in
material the wiki already covers heavily. **Verified in this session:** H. Tracy Hall co-founded
MegaDiamond in 1966 with BYU chemical engineering professor Bill Pope and Duane Horton; Louis Pope
managed MegaDiamond's plant for five years, then founded US Synthetic in Orem in April 1978 with
his father Bill as president. The company nearly failed in the mid-1980s — workforce down from 13
to six, executives unpaid, Louis Pope's house in foreclosure — before pivoting from grinding-wheel
grit to polycrystalline diamond cutters for oil and gas drill bits, after which it became the
world's leading PDC maker and one of the fastest-growing companies in the US three years running.
Bill Pope founded a third company, **Diamicron**, at 75, applying the same diamond technology to
hip joints.

The wiki has seven pages on Hall and his presses and mentions MegaDiamond, but has nothing on the
industrial lineage that came out of it. That lineage — Utah high-pressure physics to the drill bits
used across the global oil industry to diamond-surfaced orthopedic implants — is exactly the kind
of permanence-and-breadth story the charter weights highest. https://ussynthetic.com —
**not in the wiki.** MegaDiamond, Diamicron, Louis Pope, and Bill Pope are all also missing.

### Linux Networx — Bluffdale
2003 Hardware/Firmware Development medalist. The claim to check: it was one of the first companies
to commercialize Linux-based commodity cluster supercomputing, building large systems for US
national laboratories during the period when clusters displaced custom vector machines as the
default architecture for scientific computing. If that holds, it is a Utah company sitting at a
genuine inflection point in how the world does computation. Company no longer operating — this will
need Wayback and DOE procurement records rather than a live site. **Not in the wiki.**

### Bunnell Incorporated — Salt Lake City
2008 Medical Innovation medalist. Makes the Life Pulse high-frequency jet ventilator used on
premature infants with severe lung disease. Check the claims about it being the only FDA-approved
device of its type and about survival outcomes in neonatal intensive care. A small Utah company
whose device is standard equipment in NICUs would be high depth over a long horizon.
**Not in the wiki.**

### Gregory Hudnall / Hope Squad — Provo
2003 Education medalist as an administrator. The claim to check: after responding to a series of
student suicides as a Provo school administrator, Hudnall developed the Hope Squad peer
suicide-prevention model, which has since been adopted by schools across many states and into
Canada. If the adoption numbers hold, this is a Utah-origin public health intervention with real
national breadth — and it sits directly alongside the SafeUT lead in the 2020–2026 file.
**Not in the wiki.**

### Wirthlin Worldwide — Salt Lake City
2003 International Business medalist. Richard Wirthlin was Ronald Reagan's pollster and strategist;
his Utah-based firm pioneered values-based political polling and later became a global market
research company. Check the claim about the "Values in Strategy Assessment" method and its
influence on how campaigns and brands are researched. High breadth, contested depth, and a Utah
company that shaped American political practice. **Not in the wiki.**

### ARUP Laboratories — Salt Lake City
2003 and 2006 Analytical Laboratory medalist. A national clinical reference laboratory owned by the
University of Utah, one of the largest in the country, sending esoteric test results to hospitals
nationwide and returning its margin to the medical school. The ownership structure is as
interesting as the scale. Currently the wiki mentions ARUP only in passing on `myriad-genetics.md`.
https://www.aruplab.com — **needs its own page.**

### Apa Sherpa — Draper
2009 Sports & Recreation **statue** winner — the top award in his division, over every other
medalist. Held the record for most Everest summits, and lives in Utah, where the Apa Sherpa
Foundation funds education in the Khumbu so that children have alternatives to portering. A
statue-level winner who is a globally recognized figure and a Utah resident is an unusual
combination and an obvious gap. **Not in the wiki.**

### Catherine deVries, M.D. — Salt Lake City
2003 Humanitarian medalist. Pediatric urologist at the University of Utah who founded IVUMed, which
trains surgeons in low-income countries rather than performing missions itself — "teach one, reach
many." A deliberate leverage model for global surgery. Note for whoever writes it: do not confuse
her with William DeVries, the artificial-heart surgeon the wiki already covers.
**Not in the wiki.**

### Millenniata / M-DISC — Springville
2008 Computer and Software Providers medalist. Developed the M-DISC, an optical disc using an
inorganic recording layer intended to survive a thousand years, which was subsequently supported by
mainstream optical drive manufacturers. Company is gone; the format persists. A Utah answer to
long-term data permanence is on-charter for a wiki that weights permanence.
**Not in the wiki.**

### Altiris — Lindon
2003 and 2007 Software Development medalist. Systems management software; acquired by Symantec in
2007 in one of the larger Utah software exits of that era and a formative company for the Utah
County software cluster that produced much of what the wiki already documents.
**Not in the wiki.**

### Wasatch Microfluidics (now Carterra) — Salt Lake City
2007 Analytical Laboratory medalist. University of Utah spinout building array-based surface
plasmon resonance instruments for high-throughput antibody characterization, now used in
pharmaceutical discovery. Real instrumentation science with a live commercial company behind it.
https://carterra-bio.com — **not in the wiki.**

### Imagine Learning — Provo
2008 Software Development medalist. Language and literacy software for English learners, which grew
into one of the larger K-12 courseware companies in the country. The Utah edtech cluster is
under-documented in the wiki relative to its size. **Not in the wiki.**

### CompHealth — Salt Lake City
2003 Employment/Talent Services medalist. Check the claim that it originated the locum tenens
staffing model in American medicine — placing temporary physicians, particularly into rural
hospitals that otherwise could not staff a service line. If the origination claim holds, it changed
how rural American healthcare gets staffed. **Not in the wiki.**

### Globus Relief — Salt Lake City
2007 Community Development **statue** winner. Recovers surplus medical supplies and equipment from
the US healthcare system and distributes them to clinics in low-income countries. A statue-level
winner operating a genuine logistics business in the humanitarian space. **Not in the wiki.**

### Orbit Irrigation Products — Bountiful / North Salt Lake
2007 Inventor and 2008 Wholesale Goods medalist. Check the claim that it is among the largest
manufacturers of residential irrigation products in the world. Unglamorous, very high unit volume,
and directly connected to Western water use — which makes it more interesting for this wiki, not
less. **Not in the wiki.**

## Cluster leads new to this era

The seven cluster leads in the 2020–2026 file all still stand. Five more that only the pre-2020
data makes visible.

1. **The Utah diamond lineage.** Hall's presses (1955, and the wiki has seven pages on them) →
   MegaDiamond (1966, Hall + Bill Pope + Duane Horton) → US Synthetic (1978, Louis and Bill Pope) →
   Diamicron (diamond orthopedic bearings). Sixty years, one materials technology, three companies,
   two industries. The wiki documents the beginning of this chain thoroughly and the rest not at
   all. Highest-value single item in this file.
2. **The troubled-teen industry.** ~~Alpine Academy won Youth Treatment Center eight years running,
   and it is one of dozens of Utah residential programs for adolescents.~~ **Ingested 2026-08-11** as
   `utah-congregate-care-oversight-reform.md` (`Type: work`), written from the two enrolled statutes
   and their legislative status records at `le.utah.gov` plus the APM Reports / Salt Lake Tribune /
   KUER inspection-data reporting — six source pages, four of them primary tier. What the documents
   changed about this lead:

   - **The lead undercounted the reform and named the wrong end of it.** SB 127 (2021) is act one;
     **S.B. 297 (2025)** — signed 2025-03-19, effective 2025-07-01 — created a congregate care
     ombudsman, an advisory committee that sets minimum safety requirements by rule, state review of
     each program's admissions criteria, and whistleblower protection. Any page written from SB 127
     alone describes a superseded regime.
   - **The ombudsman is *not* independent**, contrary to how 2025 reporting framed it: the enrolled
     text creates the position "within the department" and requires it to refer every report back to
     that department. The distinction is on the page in both directions.
   - **The advisory committee does not set the rules** — corrected 2026-08-11 after an adversarial pass.
     Section 26B-2-124.1 gives all four powers (defining levels of care, adopting minimum safety
     requirements by rule, approving or denying admissions criteria, designating each program's level) to
     the **Office of Licensing**, "in consultation with the committee". The office also appoints all eight
     committee members, the division director picks its chair, and the division staffs it. Only one seat
     is reserved for a community representative, who may be a former resident, a parent, *or* a current
     or former owner or staff member.
   - **S.B. 297 did not narrow complaint-record disclosure** — also corrected after audit, and the most
     instructive error in this batch. Subsection 26B-2-709(6) was already law before the bill (it is in
     the version renumbered by Laws of Utah 2024, Chapter 267); enrolled copies reprint whole sections,
     so its presence proved nothing. What the bill *does* change in that section is sharper anyway: it
     drops the bar on investigating anonymous complaints and adds a **48-month staleness bar** for all
     providers, which matters in an industry whose worst allegations surface years later from former
     residents who are adults by then.
   - **"Announced inspections" belongs to the regulator, not the reporters.** APM Reports establishes
     annual checklist inspections; that they were *announced* comes from Amanda Slater, the state's top
     regulator of these programs for three years, quoted in KUER — a stronger provenance than the
     ambient version. She also says licensing directors had previously described their role as
     "technical assistance".
   - **Do not divide the enforcement numbers.** "Cited programs more than 200 times" (2021) and "just
     four violations" (2016) are different units the reporting never reconciles; an earlier draft turned
     them into a fiftyfold increase.
   - **"Eight years running" does not survive the wiki's own TSV.** The recovered rows give Alpine
     Academy medals for 2010, 2011, 2012, 2013, 2014, 2016, 2017, and 2019 — eight years, but not
     consecutive, and 2008 is a *Principal* award to an individual rather than a program medal. Also
     worth noting: the category sat under **Merchandising & Consumer Services** — now confirmed in a
     captured document, since the 2026 winners page files Youth Treatment Center under that division and
     names Odyssey House of Utah as its medalist. The overlap between Alpine Academy's run and the era
     when state inspectors found deficiencies 1.6 percent of the time is the remaining page here, and it
     still needs a captured winner document before anyone asserts an Alpine Academy medal.
   - **The measurement the charter demands exists, and it is brutal**: 670 inspections, 53,000+ items
     assessed, 861 deficiencies, programs marked compliant more than 98 percent of the time, four
     violations found in all of 2016. That is what made this writable rather than essayistic.

   Utah remains the national center of this industry, per the same reporting; the count of programs is
   still 2021-vintage journalism and is the cheapest remaining improvement (`dlbc.utah.gov` publishes
   a facility search and monthly reports; neither is captured).
3. **Utah scrapbooking and craft direct-selling.** Karen Foster Design won the 2007 Production
   statue; Stampin' Up!, Close To My Heart, and Jeanette Lynton all medaled. The modern scrapbooking
   industry was substantially built in Utah County, and it is a distinct phenomenon from the
   supplements/essential-oils direct-selling cluster already noted — different products, same
   distribution innovation.
4. **Utah as a call-center and back-office state.** eBay in Draper, Discover in West Valley, ACS,
   Stampin' Up!'s customer servicing operation, Visa. There is a real economic-geography story about
   why so much of this capacity landed in Utah — labor force, returned-missionary language skills,
   power and land costs — and no page anywhere on it.
5. **Engineering firms as invisible infrastructure.** Spectrum Engineers, Reaveley, Van Boerum &
   Frank, VCBO, FFKR, Colvin, Hansen Allen & Luce appear year after year across four decades of
   Utah building. Individually unremarkable, collectively they designed most of the built
   environment the wiki's subjects work inside — including seismic retrofits in a state with a
   significant earthquake exposure.

  are the majority of all 3,975 rows.

  from the Archive. Craig Jessop is listed above because he is independently identifiable; the
  other ten need a different source before they are worth anything.
