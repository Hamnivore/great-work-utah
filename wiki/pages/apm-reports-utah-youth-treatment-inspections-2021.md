## Summary

Reporting by Will Craft and Jessica Schreifels, published nine days after the Utah House passed
[S.B. 127](utah-sb127-2021-congregate-care-statute.md) and twelve days before the governor signed it.
It is the closest thing this wiki holds to a measurement of the *pre-reform* regulatory baseline: APM
Reports and The Salt Lake Tribune converted five years of the Office of Licensing's own inspection
checklists into data and counted the findings.

The answer was 861 deficiencies out of more than 53,000 items assessed. That is the number the 2021
statute was written against, and it is why the reform's central move was showing up more often and
unannounced rather than writing new prohibitions alone.

It is also the source for this wiki's magnitude claims about the sector's size, and those are the
weakest part of it — see the reliability notes.

## Useful Claims

- **Inspectors marked programs compliant more than 98 percent of the time.** Across 670 inspection
  reports the analysis found inspectors assessed more than 53,000 items and documented only 861
  deficiencies — a noncompliance rate the story puts at 1.6 percent.
- **Nearly half of the programs inspected had never been marked noncompliant in five years.** Of 155
  programs inspected, close to half had received no noncompliant rating in that period.
- **Findings were falling, not rising, before the reform**: 84 deficiencies in 2020 against 233 in
  2018, with 86 percent of facilities inspected in the year before publication receiving a perfect
  score — most of those inspections conducted virtually because of the pandemic.
- **Inspections were annual and checklist-driven** before 2021: once a year per program, against a
  14-page checklist that the story says is unweighted, so "a ripped van seat counts the same as a
  child complaining they aren't given enough to eat". The story does not say the visits were
  *announced* — for that, see the former Office of Licensing director quoted in
  [KUER](kuer-utah-congregate-care-reform-one-year-2022.md).
- **An outside expert called the instrument useless.** Ronald Davidson, former director of the Mental
  Health Policy Program at the University of Illinois at Chicago's Department of Psychiatry, reviewed
  a portion of the reports for the Tribune and concluded that either Utah has the nation's
  best-performing residential treatment centers or its investigators are "sleepwalking their way
  through on-site reviews".
- **The story attributes $534,000 for more inspectors to the bill.** It reports that S.B. 127
  "provides an additional $534,000 to hire more inspectors" — a figure that appears nowhere in
  [the enrolled statute](utah-sb127-2021-congregate-care-statute.md), whose front matter says it
  appropriates nothing. See the reliability notes: the two statements are in tension and this wiki has
  not resolved which vehicle carried the money.
- **It reports the sector's national concentration and scale**: that Utah has "a greater
  concentration of such programs than any other state", that more than 12,000 children from across
  the country were sent to Utah for treatment over the preceding five years, and that the industry
  "brings hundreds of millions of dollars into the state's economy each year and employs thousands of
  its residents".
- **The state published almost nothing.** Utah regulators posted only the most egregious violations
  online, in cases where they had threatened a facility's license; the story says one Utah teen
  treatment center received such a threat in the preceding two years, and it is now closed. The
  inspection reports themselves became public only through a database the Tribune, KUER, and APM
  Reports built.
- **One facility dominated the findings.** New Beginnings Behavioral Treatment, an 11-bed facility in
  a Salt Lake suburb, accounted for 14 percent of all noncompliant findings, averaging 15 deficiencies
  per checklist; its chief administrative officer's response, quoted in the story, is that the
  checklists are not "used as an indicator of performance or efficacy".
- **The bill's own sponsor doubted the transparency regime.** Sen. Mike McKell is quoted saying the
  Legislature "should take a hard look at enhancing our transparency", and that the state has not had
  enough employees inspecting.

## Reliability Notes

**Secondary tier, but the underlying records are the state's own.** The deficiency counts are the
reporters' tabulation of Office of Licensing inspection checklists — a computed result, not an agency
publication — so the arithmetic is APM Reports' responsibility and cannot be re-derived from anything
this wiki holds. The methodology is stated in the story (every residential treatment and wilderness
program, five years, converted from PDFs by a program the newsroom wrote); the underlying database
was published, and this wiki has not retrieved it. Treat the counts as well-documented journalism
rather than as a government statistic.

**The program count is internally inconsistent, and this matters for anyone citing the sector's
size.** The story's opening says "more than 100 treatment programs" and its analysis section says
"155 programs inspected". Both are quoted above without reconciliation, because the story does not
reconcile them; the likely reading is that 155 is the number appearing anywhere in five years of
records while roughly 100 were licensed at the time, but that is inference. Cite neither figure as a
current count — it is a 2021 figure either way.

**Three magnitude claims carry no source inside the story**: the national concentration
("greater concentration of such programs than any other state"), the 12,000 children over five years,
and "hundreds of millions of dollars into the state's economy each year". These are the sentences most
likely to be quoted onward and the least supported here; they are the reporters' assertions, and this
wiki attributes them rather than adopting them.

**The $534,000 for inspectors is not in the bill, and the story says it is.**
[S.B. 127's](utah-sb127-2021-congregate-care-statute.md) front matter states it appropriates nothing,
yet the story says the bill "provides" the money. Both readings are plausible — the figure may be the
bill's fiscal-note estimate of what the new duties cost, or the money may have moved in the session's
appropriations act — and nothing captured here distinguishes them. Anyone repeating the figure should
say the reporting attributes it to the reform, not that the statute appropriated it. The fiscal note
at `le.utah.gov` would settle this cheaply and has not been retrieved.

**Written before enactment.** The story says the legislation "still needs the governor's signature",
which it received on 2021-03-22 ([status record](utah-sb127-2021-bill-status-record.md)). Its
description of what the bill would do is therefore a forecast of the enrolled text, and one detail is
looser than the statute: it says programs may not use mechanical restraints "without the state's prior
authorization", which matches the statute, and that inspections rise "from one to four", which the
statute phrases as at least once each calendar quarter with at least two unannounced.

**A live capture and a 2026 snapshot both exist**, and the page is stable enough that the archived
copy and the capture agree; APM Reports has kept the story online since 2021 under its "Utah's Youth
Treatment Industry" series.
